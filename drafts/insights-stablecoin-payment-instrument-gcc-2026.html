Stablecoins as Payment Instruments: Regulatory Convergence Across the GCC, US, and EU in 2026

July 2026  ·  10 min read  ·  MENA Advisory

Stablecoins have crossed a threshold in 2026. Across seven major jurisdictions simultaneously, regulators have moved from consultation to enforcement, treating fiat-backed stablecoins as regulated payment instruments rather than speculative crypto assets. For payments professionals operating in or connected to the GCC, this is not a future-state conversation. Licensed issuers are live in the UAE, Saudi Arabia is building infrastructure, and the commercial economics are now large enough to attract serious banking-sector interest. The question is no longer whether stablecoins will form part of payments architecture. It is how quickly compliance frameworks, treasury operations, and correspondent relationships need to adapt.

The Global Regulatory Convergence

Seven jurisdictions, including the United States, European Union, United Kingdom, Singapore, Hong Kong, UAE, and Japan, now mandate full reserve backing, licensed issuers, and guaranteed redemption rights for stablecoins used as payment instruments. The speed of convergence in the first half of 2026 is without precedent in payments regulation.

In the United States, the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for US Stablecoins Act, known as the GENIUS Act, now governs a market capitalised at $314.68 billion. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency issued its proposed rule on 25 February 2026, covering nationally chartered banks wishing to issue or custody stablecoins. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation followed with its own proposal for insured depository institutions operating through subsidiaries. On 9 April 2026, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network and the Office of Foreign Assets Control jointly issued a notice of proposed rulemaking implementing the GENIUS Act's anti-money-laundering and sanctions compliance requirements. Under that rule, permitted payment stablecoin issuers, or PPSIs, are classified as financial institutions under the Bank Secrecy Act, triggering mandatory AML programme requirements, suspicious activity reporting, and recordkeeping obligations consistent with those applied to banks. Final implementation rules are targeted for 18 July 2026, with full enforcement expected from January 2027.

In Europe, the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, or MiCA, has been operative since June 2024, but the transitional enforcement period for stablecoin issuers expired on 2 March 2026, confirmed by the European Banking Authority in February. Euro-denominated stablecoins structured as electronic money tokens, or EMTs, must now hold an e-money licence and maintain 100 percent liquid reserves. The interface between MiCA's token regime and existing payments directives creates compliance complexity that European issuers and their GCC partners are still resolving.

The commercial stakes are material. Circle's Q2 2026 results reported $668 million of reserve income alongside $412 million of distribution and transaction costs. That ratio explains why retail banks, sovereign wealth vehicles, and payments groups across the GCC have intensified their stablecoin strategy work in the past six months.

The UAE: The GCC's Live, Licensed Market

The UAE is the most operationally advanced stablecoin jurisdiction in the GCC and among the most advanced globally. The Central Bank of the UAE regulated fiat-backed stablecoins under its Payment Token Services Regulation, effective August 2024, which classifies payment tokens as crypto assets fully backed by one or more fiat currencies and used for settlement or value transfer. The transition period under that regulation ended on 16 June 2026, placing the framework in its full enforcement phase. Any entity that issues, redeems, or facilitates payment tokens in the UAE mainland must now hold a Central Bank licence. Algorithmic stablecoins and privacy tokens are prohibited.

Alongside the federal framework, the Dubai Financial Services Authority and Abu Dhabi Global Market's Financial Services Regulatory Authority operate their own regimes within their respective free zones. The DFSA's crypto token recognition framework and ADGM's digital assets regime have each granted recognition to specific tokens, creating a layered but increasingly coordinated structure. The practical implication for payments firms is that a single stablecoin product operating across UAE mainland and free zone entities may require engagement with up to three regulators, each with distinct disclosure, redemption, and custody requirements.

AED-backed stablecoin issuance is now commercially live, with licensed issuers operating under CBUAE oversight. This opens a genuinely new settlement rail for domestic retail payments, corporate treasury, and remittance applications within the UAE.

Saudi Arabia and the Wider GCC

Saudi Arabia has moved from policy intent to infrastructure build. The Saudi Central Bank, known as SAMA, has published its digital assets regulatory framework and is understood to be in active dialogue with prospective issuers of SAR-denominated stablecoins. The Vision 2030 Financial Sector Development Programme explicitly references programmable money and digital settlement infrastructure as priority areas. SAMA's participation in Project mBridge, the multi-central-bank digital currency platform developed with the Bank for International Settlements, provides a parallel track: stablecoins for commercial use and a CBDC rail for wholesale interbank settlement are being developed as complementary rather than competing instruments.

Qatar, Bahrain, and Kuwait each have crypto asset frameworks at various stages of maturity, but none has yet activated a licensed stablecoin issuance regime equivalent to the CBUAE's. The Bahrain Central Bank has been among the earlier movers in the region on digital asset licensing generally, and the framework for payment tokens is expected to be updated in the second half of 2026.

For GCC payments professionals, the regional picture creates both an opportunity and a compliance asymmetry. A stablecoin issued and licensed in the UAE is not automatically recognised in Saudi Arabia or Qatar, and cross-border stablecoin flows across GCC jurisdictions currently lack a harmonised passporting mechanism equivalent to SEPA's framework for e-money in Europe.

Cross-Border Corridors and the Remittance Case

The GCC generated an estimated $131.5 billion in outbound remittance flows in 2025, with the South Asia and Southeast Asia corridors accounting for the largest share by volume. These corridors are precisely where stablecoin settlement has the clearest near-term commercial proposition: reducing correspondent banking costs, compressing settlement from one to two days to near-real-time, and providing price transparency to senders.

USDC and USDT are already being used informally for value transfer across the UAE-India and UAE-Philippines corridors, operating in a regulatory grey area that the CBUAE's enforcement posture is now directly addressing. Licensed, AML-compliant stablecoin rails present an opportunity to formalise and scale what is already occurring informally, but only where the receiving jurisdiction has a compatible framework for stablecoin receipt and conversion. India's regulatory position on foreign-issued stablecoins remains restrictive, which limits the corridor opportunity even where UAE issuance is fully licensed.

The SWIFT cooperative's retail cross-border framework, updated in 2025, now contemplates interoperability with regulated digital asset networks, which opens a path for stablecoin settlement to connect with existing correspondent infrastructure rather than displace it entirely.

What Payments Firms Should Do Now

The window for treating stablecoins as a watch-and-wait topic has closed. Firms operating in or connected to the GCC should take the following steps in the immediate term.

First, conduct a regulatory mapping exercise. If your firm issues, distributes, or settles using stablecoins across UAE mainland and free zone entities, you need a clear view of which regulatory bodies have jurisdiction over each activity and whether current licences cover each use case. The CBUAE's June 2026 enforcement activation means that unlicensed facilitation is now an active compliance risk, not a future one.

Second, assess reserve and liquidity requirements against your current treasury structure. The GENIUS Act's PPSI rules and MiCA's EMT requirements both mandate high-quality liquid assets in specific proportions. If your firm has correspondent relationships with US or European licensed issuers, their compliance obligations will flow into your contractual arrangements.

Third, review AML and sanctions compliance programmes for stablecoin-specific transaction monitoring. The FinCEN and OFAC joint rule of 9 April 2026 sets a clear expectation that PPSI-connected entities maintain monitoring capabilities equivalent to those applied to bank transfers. Existing crypto transaction monitoring tools may require calibration for stablecoin-specific risk typologies, including chain-hopping, mixer use, and cross-jurisdictional layering.

Fourth, engage proactively with the cross-border passporting gap in the GCC. In the absence of a regional harmonisation mechanism, firms with operations across multiple GCC states need bilateral legal opinions on whether a CBUAE-licensed stablecoin product can be distributed or used for settlement in each target market. This is not a theoretical point: it has direct implications for product structuring, counterparty agreements, and capital allocation.

The commercial case for stablecoin payment rails in the GCC is strong. The regulatory frameworks to support that case are now largely in place in the UAE and developing rapidly elsewhere. Firms that complete the compliance groundwork in the next two quarters will be positioned to move quickly when the remaining GCC jurisdictions finalise their regimes. Those that do not will find themselves restructuring arrangements under time pressure.
